# Flood and water incidents

Orientation only. Your unit's own SOP and your incident commander outrank everything on this
page. Nothing here authorises an entry.

## What a drowning-imminent row implies operationally

`DROWNING_IMMINENT` is a minutes-scale clock. It is the one severity that justifies moving
before the picture is complete, and it is the reason severity sorts above evidence quality on
this board.

A drowning-imminent row that is also `confirmed — first-hand` is the strongest thing this
system can say. Treat a drowning-imminent row that is only testimony as a reason to get eyes
on it fast — a nearer team, a relay moved closer — rather than as a reason to hold.

## Order of preference for a rescue attempt

The long-standing order, least to most risk to the rescuer: **reach, throw, row, go**.

- **Reach** with a pole, branch or line from a stable position.
- **Throw** a line or a floating device.
- **Row** — reach them from a boat rather than from the water.
- **Go** — entering the water is the last option, for trained swiftwater rescuers with
  appropriate protection and a plan for getting both people out.

An untrained rescuer entering moving water usually adds a second casualty. Two casualties is
worse for the board and for the person you went in for.

## Moving water

Water in a flood is not a still pond with a current. It carries debris, it undercuts what you
are standing on, and it hides holes, drains and fences.

- Shallow-looking moving water knocks adults off their feet at surprisingly low depth; once
  down, footing is very hard to regain.
- Never walk or drive into water of unknown depth over a road, culvert or bridge deck.
- Vehicles are buoyant far earlier than people expect and are lost long before the water
  looks deep.
- Work upstream-aware: assume anything that gets loose upstream arrives where you are.

## Personal protection

Flotation for everyone working near the edge, not just those in a boat. Helmet, footwear that
stays on, and a whistle. Never tie a line to a person in a way that cannot be released under
load.

Floodwater is contaminated. Cover wounds, and record who went in — decontamination and
follow-up matter later even when nobody feels hurt.

## Rooftops, upper floors and rising water

Rising-water incidents concentrate people at the top of the structure. On this board that
often means a zone tag naming a `roof` or a high floor, or an `UNPLACED` row from someone who
never entered a zone at all.

Prioritise by how fast the water is rising against where the person is, not by how loud the
report is. Someone on a roof with the water still below the eaves has more time than someone
on a stairwell landing — even when the second report is quieter.

## Evidence flags that recur here

- `rushing water` — the phone's classifier matched a broadband water-like signal near that
  device. It is not a depth measurement and not a flow rate.
- `enclosed / dark` with `rushing water` is worth attention: it is consistent with someone in
  a stairwell, basement, vehicle or under a collapsed section with water present.
- `pinned` with water present is the worst combination on this board — a person who cannot
  self-evacuate as the level rises. It justifies asking for a technical rescue capability
  early rather than sending a general team first and escalating later.
